• ## The Kingdom Small Enough to Count

  Once there was a queen whose kingdom was filled with ingenious machines.

  There were mills that shared wheels, gates that required iron keys, wells whose buckets descended and returned long after they were lowered, and messengers who ran between them carrying records of everything that occurred. No single person could see the whole kingdom at once.

  At the palace lived a young oracle. The oracle never visited the mills or gates. It sat in a windowless room and received only the messengers’ ribbons. After reading each ribbon, it foretold what the next ribbon would say.

  Its predictions became remarkably good.

  Soon the court divided into two camps. One said, “The oracle understands the kingdom.” The other said, “It understands nothing. It has merely learned the habits of messengers.”

  The queen summoned an old systems scholar.

  “Which camp is right?” she asked.

  The scholar considered this and replied, “Your Majesty, the question cannot yet be answered.”

  Both camps were offended.

  “The kingdom is too large,” the scholar explained. “When the oracle is wrong, we do not know whether it failed to understand something the ribbons revealed, or whether the messengers never revealed it. When it is right, we do not know whether it understood the machinery or merely guessed from some accidental mark upon a ribbon. Before we judge the
  oracle, we need a kingdom whose truth we can know.”

  So the scholar built a second kingdom.

  It was the smallest kingdom that still behaved like the first.

  It had four workers, two workbenches, two iron keys, and one well. A worker could labor only while seated at a bench. Some tasks required a key. If the key was held, the worker waited in a line. The well could hold only two descending buckets; a third worker arriving too soon had to wait. A steward chose which worker moved next, sometimes by rule and
  sometimes by an honest cast of dice.

  The little kingdom was simple enough that every possible condition of it could be written in a great book.

  The scholar knew who sat at each bench, who held each key, the order of every waiting line, which buckets were in the well, and the exact chance of every event that might happen next. When the steward cast a die, the scholar did not merely record what happened. Two accountants followed every branch the die might have chosen.

  The first accountant began at the kingdom’s first morning and traced every possible history forward.

  The second never looked back. After each messenger arrived, she took yesterday’s possibilities and filtered away everything the new ribbon made impossible.

  They worked by different methods and were forbidden to copy from one another. Whenever their answers failed to agree, the experiment stopped. The queen had asked for an instrument, not another oracle.

  Then the scholar appointed four messengers.

  The first reported only what kind of event had occurred and which worker had acted.

  The second also named the key, bench, or well involved.

  The third named the other worker affected: the owner of a contested key, or the worker awakened from a line.

  The fourth carried the lineage of every bucket, so that an issue and its later return could be joined even after other events intervened.

  Each messenger’s ribbon had the same shape. What a messenger was forbidden to reveal appeared as a blank seal, so that the length of the ribbon itself could not whisper the answer.

  The young oracle was then taught from many histories of the little kingdom. Its task remained modest: read the ribbons and predict the next one.

  At first, the scholar gave it every ribbon from the kingdom’s morning bell onward.

  Something unexpected happened.

  Even the poorest messenger’s ribbons often identified the kingdom’s condition perfectly. The acting workers, the order of events, and the visible consequences together left only one possible history. The accountants repeatedly placed a single pebble on the table: one surviving state, with probability one.

  “The oracle has learned everything!” cried one faction.

  “The experiment has failed!” cried the other.

  The scholar said neither.

  Instead, she shortened the chronicle.

  The oracle now received only the most recent ribbons. An iron key might have been acquired before the surviving record began. A bucket might already have been descending when the first visible messenger arrived. Sometimes the morning bell itself had fallen beyond the edge of memory, leaving the oracle uncertain not only about what had happened, but
  about where in the kingdom’s day it had awakened.

  The accountants’ bowls grew fat with pebbles.

  Several different conditions of the kingdom now produced exactly the same visible ribbons. Some pebbles were heavy and some light, because the steward’s laws made certain hidden histories more likely than others. But no listener confined to those ribbons—oracle, scholar, queen, or god—could honestly select one pebble when the evidence supported
  several.

  Only then was the instrument ready.

  For every prediction, the scholar could ask three different questions.

  First: What knowledge did the ribbons permit?

  The two accountants answered this exactly. Their weighted pebbles described everything any perfect listener could infer from the available record.

  Second: How much of that permitted knowledge had the oracle organized within itself?

  The scholar examined whether distinctions present in the accountants’ book could also be recovered from the oracle’s changing internal patterns.

  Third: How much of what the oracle carried actually reached its prophecy?

  Some distinctions might matter within the oracle yet remain absent from its next spoken answer. Others might be spoken without having been organized in any durable way. The scholar tested these separately.

  Now, when the oracle failed, the court could locate the failure.

  If the messengers had made two worlds indistinguishable, the fault did not belong to the oracle.

  If the ribbons distinguished them but the oracle did not, the loss occurred in learning.

  If the oracle carried the distinction but its prophecy did not reveal or use it, the loss occurred at the final window through which the court observed the oracle.

  The four messengers could be compared without changing the kingdom underneath them. The chronicle could be shortened or restored. Workers could exchange names each morning. Independent mills could be coupled through a shared key. The oracle could be made larger or taught from more histories. In every case, the accountants remained beside it, stating
  the best that the evidence itself allowed.

  Years later, the queen again asked the scholar, “Does the oracle understand the kingdom?”

  The scholar replied, “I can give you something better than a word whose meaning changes with whoever speaks it.”

  She opened the accountants’ book.

  “I can show you what the kingdom surrendered to the messengers, what the messengers surrendered to the oracle, and what the oracle surrendered to us.”

  And because the little kingdom was small enough to count, no one at court had to take her answer on faith.

  That little kingdom is Yupi. Its purpose is not to prove that a transformer possesses a world model, nor to catch one pretending. It is to build a world exact enough that we can measure where knowledge becomes unavailable—between world and trace, trace and learned representation, and representation and visible prediction—without blaming a learner for
  secrets it was never told.
