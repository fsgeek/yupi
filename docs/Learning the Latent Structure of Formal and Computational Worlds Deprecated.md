# Learning the Latent Structure of Formal and Computational Worlds

## Motivation

Transformers are most commonly studied as models of natural language, code, and increasingly biological sequences. In each case, next-token prediction produces internal representations that appear to capture structure far richer than local token statistics.

It remains unclear, however, how much of this behavior depends on properties peculiar to language-like corpora and how much follows more generally from training transformers on observations generated by structured systems.

This project proposes to study transformers trained from scratch on deliberately small, non-linguistic worlds whose underlying semantics are fully known. Rather than optimizing models for a downstream task, the central question is:

> **When a transformer is trained only to predict observations produced by a structured world, what representation of that world's hidden structure does it learn?**

Two initial domains provide complementary testbeds:

1. **Synthetic computational systems**, where observable event traces are generated by hidden machine state and transition rules.
2. **Small formal mathematical systems**, where sequences arise from axioms, inference rules, and evolving proof states.

Because both worlds are constructed by us, their complete underlying state is available as ground truth. This permits unusually direct comparison between a transformer's latent representation and the actual system that generated its training data.

## Research Questions

The project will investigate four primary questions.

**RQ1 — Structural recovery.**  
What properties of the generating system become recoverable from transformer hidden states after next-token pretraining alone?

For a computational system, examples include process state, resource ownership, scheduler state, page residency, dependency relationships, and outstanding operations.

For a formal mathematical system, examples include proof state, equivalence classes, applicability of inference rules, and structural relationships between expressions.

**RQ2 — Emergence and scale.**  
At what model and dataset scales do particular structural representations emerge?

Rather than assuming larger models are necessarily better, models spanning approximately 1M–100M parameters will be compared to identify capability thresholds and saturation points.

**RQ3 — Representation versus nomenclature.**  
Does the model recover equivalent latent structure when the representational alphabet is semantically opaque?

Experiments will compare descriptive tokens such as `READ`, `LOCK`, and `SCHEDULE` with randomly renamed symbols such as `X17`, `Q4`, and `M22`.

If similar internal organization emerges under renaming, this provides stronger evidence that the model is learning relations inherent in the generating process rather than semantic hints encoded by the experimenter.

**RQ4 — Sensitivity to world semantics.**  
How do learned representations change when the underlying generative rules change while the observable alphabet remains constant?

Examples include changing scheduler policy, resource-allocation rules, transition probabilities, or a mathematical axiom or inference rule.

This allows us to ask whether the transformer's representation primarily captures surface regularities or the deeper semantics responsible for those observations.

## Experimental System I: Synthetic Computational World

A small machine or operating-system-like simulator will generate event streams from explicit hidden state.

The internal state might include:

- processes and threads;
- runnable, blocked, and executing states;
- CPUs;
- files and handles;
- memory mappings and pages;
- locks and ownership;
- asynchronous I/O;
- devices and interrupts;
- queues and scheduler state.

The transformer will not receive this state directly.

Instead, it observes an event stream drawn from a deliberately restricted alphabet, for example:

`CREATE`, `OPEN`, `READ`, `WRITE`, `MAP`, `FAULT`, `BLOCK`, `WAKE`, `SCHEDULE`, `IRQ`, `LOCK`, `UNLOCK`.

Entities will receive symbolic identifiers such as `P3`, `F7`, `CPU1`, and `PAGE4`.

A representative trace might resemble:

`CREATE P1`  
`OPEN P1 F2`  
`READ P1 F2`  
`BLOCK P1 O4`  
`IRQ D1`  
`WAKE P1 O4`  
`SCHEDULE CPU0 P1`

The simulator will simultaneously retain the exact hidden state after every event.

This creates paired data:

**observable history ↔ true machine state**

without exposing the latter during pretraining.

After training, lightweight probes can test whether hidden states encode properties such as:

- whether a process is runnable;
- which process owns a lock;
- whether a page is resident;
- whether an I/O operation is outstanding;
- which resource caused a process to block;
- which events share a common causal dependency.

The model can additionally be tested on impossible or perturbed traces to determine whether learned likelihood corresponds to semantic validity.

## Experimental System II: Formal Mathematical World

A second generator will construct sequences from a deliberately restricted formal system.

Candidate starting domains include:

- propositional logic;
- Boolean algebra;
- finite groups;
- term-rewriting systems;
- small equational calculi;
- elementary graph transformations.

The initial system should be simple enough that complete proof state and semantic validity are mechanically available, while rich enough to permit many alternative derivations.

Training sequences will represent activity within the formal system rather than conventional natural-language theorem statements.

The model may observe expressions and legal transformations but will not receive an explicit representation of proof state beyond what appears in the sequence.

Post-training probes can ask whether hidden states encode:

- equivalence between expressions;
- available inference rules;
- distance to a valid conclusion;
- classes of structurally related terms;
- whether a derivation remains satisfiable;
- hidden invariants imposed by the formal system.

A proof checker or independent evaluator provides an exact external oracle.

## Model Design

The first phase deliberately avoids pretrained models.

A small decoder-only transformer will be implemented and trained from scratch. Initial configurations might span approximately:

- 1–2M parameters;
- 5M;
- 10M;
- 25M;
- 50M;
- 100M.

Sequence lengths are expected to remain modest, allowing these experiments to fit comfortably on a single contemporary workstation GPU.

The purpose of the scaling experiment is not to maximize benchmark performance but to identify where particular forms of structural representation first become detectable.

Architecture, tokenizer, optimizer, training loop, checkpointing, evaluation, and corpus generation will all remain under experimental control.

## Experimental Matrix

The project will vary at least four dimensions independently:

**Model capacity**  
Small through moderately sized transformers.

**Training-data volume**  
Fractions of the generated corpus, allowing data-limited and capacity-limited regimes to be distinguished.

**World complexity**  
Increasing numbers of entities, rules, interactions, and hidden-state dimensions.

**Semantic transparency of the alphabet**  
Human-readable versus randomly renamed tokens.

This permits construction of empirical surfaces such as:

`representation quality = f(model size, dataset size, world complexity)`

rather than reporting the performance of a single trained model.

## Evaluation

Next-token loss will be treated as only one metric.

The primary evaluation concerns latent structure.

Possible measures include:

1. Linear-probe accuracy for known hidden-state variables.
2. Nonlinear probe performance where appropriate.
3. Representation similarity across models and random token renamings.
4. Generalization to unseen combinations of familiar components.
5. Detection of impossible sequences.
6. Sensitivity to changes in the world's transition rules.
7. Ability to predict longer-horizon consequences.
8. Emergence thresholds as model and dataset size increase.

Where possible, probe complexity will be constrained so that successful decoding indicates information already organized within the representation rather than learned primarily by the probe.

## Key Controls

Several controls are particularly important.

**Random token renaming.**  
Tests whether representation depends on human-readable semantics.

**Shuffled sequences.**  
Preserves token frequencies while destroying temporal and causal structure.

**Transition-preserving synthetic baselines.**  
Allows comparison against simpler Markov or recurrent models.

**Held-out compositions.**  
Tests whether models generalize relationships rather than memorize trajectories.

**World-rule perturbation.**  
Changes semantics without changing the representational alphabet.

**Model-family comparison.**  
A later phase could compare transformers against state-space models, recurrent networks, or graph-based approaches.

## Hypotheses

A preregistered version could test several competing hypotheses.

**H1: Surface-statistics hypothesis.**  
Next-token prediction primarily captures local transition regularities and does not reliably recover deeper hidden state.

**H2: Predictive-state hypothesis.**  
Models learn compressed representations of hidden variables insofar as those variables improve prediction of future observations.

**H3: Structural abstraction hypothesis.**  
Beyond a threshold of data and capacity, models develop representations corresponding to relational structure that generalize across entity identities and token renamings.

**H4: Semantic-model hypothesis.**  
Models construct representations sensitive to the generating rules themselves; changing those rules causes systematic reorganization even when the observable vocabulary is held constant.

These hypotheses need not be mutually exclusive. Different forms of representation may emerge at different scales.

## Why This Matters

Most mechanistic study of transformer representations examines models trained on corpora whose true generative structure is inaccessible.

Natural language does not provide ground truth for its latent causal state. Neither do biological corpora or source-code repositories.

Synthetic formal and computational worlds provide something unusual:

> **the complete generative process is known.**

This makes it possible to ask not merely whether a model performs well, but whether its internal representation corresponds to meaningful structure in the world that generated its observations.

The work could therefore contribute to several areas:

- understanding representation formation in transformers;
- scaling behavior in small specialized models;
- predictive-state representation;
- mechanistic interpretability;
- learned models of computational systems;
- formal reasoning;
- anomaly detection and systems observability.

The broader question is whether next-token prediction should be understood primarily as language modelling or as a more general mechanism for discovering predictive abstractions in structured sequential processes.

## Initial Scope

The first implementation should remain deliberately small.

Phase 1 would use one synthetic computational environment, one formal system, and transformer models no larger than approximately 25M parameters.

The goal would be to establish:

1. a complete from-scratch pretraining pipeline;
2. controlled corpus generators;
3. reproducible scaling experiments;
4. probes against known latent state;
5. token-renaming and shuffled-sequence controls.

Only after establishing those foundations would the project expand toward richer operating-system traces, larger mathematical domains, or naturally occurring systems data.

The first milestone is therefore not “build a systems foundation model.”

It is:

> **Demonstrate whether a small transformer trained purely on observations can recover latent structure from a world whose semantics we know exactly.**
